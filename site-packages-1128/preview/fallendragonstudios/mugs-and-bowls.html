Stoneware mug

The four glazes

Every piece on this page comes in any of four glazes at the same price. All four are mixed in the studio and fired to cone 6. The test tiles show how each one sits over the clay.

  • Rocket Pool

    Our signature: a deep turquoise that moves in the kiln. It slides down walls and into the bottoms of bowls and the wells of plates, setting darker and glassier where it lies thick and breaking rust brown where it runs thin. Rocket Pool loads are fired with plenty of air, because its copper only stays turquoise that way.

  • Celadon

    A pale blue-green, translucent enough that throwing lines show through like ripples under water. The color comes from a trace of iron fired in reduction, with the kiln starved of air near the top of the firing.

  • Tenmoku

    Near black and glossy, breaking to a warm rust wherever the glaze thins over a rim, a handle edge or a throwing line. Also a reduction glaze, and the most forgiving of coffee stains.

  • Chalk white

    A satin white that stops short of the foot and leaves a band of dark bare clay at the bottom of the piece. Quiet beside the other three, which is why people mix it into a set.

What never leaves the studio

A piece with a crawl, the bare patch left when glaze pulls back in the fire, on any surface your spoon or lip will touch. Those pots are broken up and the clay is reclaimed. The same goes for a plate that rocks or a mug with a hairline at the handle.

From wet clay to your table

  1. ThrowingA weighed ball of stoneware clay is centered on the wheel and pulled up into a wall in three or four passes. Throwing rings are left in on purpose: they catch glaze.
  2. TrimmingNext day the pot is leather-hard. It goes back on the wheel upside down, and a loop tool carves the foot ring and takes weight out of the base. The studio chop is pressed in beside it.
  3. BisqueA first, cooler firing turns dry clay into bisque: hard enough to handle, porous enough to soak up glaze.
  4. GlazingInsides are poured, outsides dipped. The foot ring is wiped clean so the pot does not weld itself to the kiln shelf.
  5. Glaze firingUp to cone 6, a little over 2,200 °F, then a day and a half of cooling before anyone may open the door.

Care, in plain words

Dishwasher
Fine on any rack. Hand washing keeps a glossy glaze brighter for longer.
Microwave
Fine for reheating. Stoneware holds heat, so the handle may come out warm.
Oven and stove
Not recommended. Stoneware cracks when one part heats faster than another, and a burner or a broiler does exactly that.
Hot and cold
No sudden jumps: not from the refrigerator into boiling water, not from the dishwasher into the freezer.
Gray marks
Marks left by cutlery are metal sitting on the glaze, not scratches. A paste of baking soda and water lifts them.
The foot
The unglazed foot ring is sanded smooth but is still fired clay. Lift, do not drag, on soft wood.
Chips
A chipped rim is sharp. Retire the piece from the table and let it hold pens or a plant.
