The wall-mount letterbox

Wall-mount letterbox

This is the box for a house whose mail comes to the door. The body is galvanized steel folded from one sheet, so there is no joint along the top for rain to find. A lift-up lid covers the drop slot: the carrier raises it with one finger, the mail falls behind a baffle, and the lid drops shut on its own weight.

It is a letterbox first. If most of what you get is cartons, look at the parcel drop boxes instead, or let the sizer decide.

Where a wall box works best

Fix it beside the door on the latch side, under a porch roof if you have one, with the slot about 48 inches off the floor: low enough for the carrier to see into, high enough that nobody has to stoop.

Brick, block, clapboard and vinyl siding all take it. On siding, hit a stud or the sheathing behind it; the fitting sheet shows how to find one with a knuckle and a small nail. For a duplex or a house split into apartments, hang two or three in a row, a hand-width apart, each with its own number plate above.

Mailbox post

each$69

A post has two jobs: hold the box at the right height, and survive the snowplow. This one is 2 inch square galvanized steel tube with a welded arm and a diagonal brace, finished in Iron Black. The post itself does not go into the ground. A 24 inch steel sleeve does, and the post slides into the sleeve and locks with a single bolt.

That bolt has three positions two inches apart, which puts the base of the box 41, 43 or 45 inches above the ground, so the height can be set after the concrete has hardened instead of before. And on the day a plow finally clips it, you pull the bolt, lift the post out and straighten or replace it without digging.

Stop 5 · Steel and finish

What the boxes are made of

Every box starts as galvanized steel sheet: steel dipped in zinc at the mill, so the rust protection is in the metal rather than painted over it. We use 18-gauge for the letterbox, 20-gauge for the curbside tunnel and 16-gauge for the parcel boxes, which get leaned on.

The color is powder coat, a dry powder sprayed onto the steel and baked until it flows into one hard skin. It is thicker than brushed paint, it does not run at the edges, and it stands up to sprinklers and July sun. Where a stone chips it, the zinc underneath keeps working until you dab the chip with the pot of matching paint in the fixings bag.

Three finishes

  • Iron Black
  • Barn Red
  • Lake Blue

Letterboxes, post-mount mailboxes and both parcel boxes come in all three. The post and the number plate come in Iron Black only, because white numerals read best on it and a post should not be the loudest thing on the street.

Fixings in every box. Screws, anchors and bolts are stainless or zinc plated, counted into a bag and taped inside the box, so nothing rattles loose in the carton and nothing sends you back to the hardware store.

Stop 6 · Fitting

Putting one up in an afternoon

A letterbox on the wall

  1. Hold the box where you want it, slot about 48 inches off the porch floor, and check it with a level.

  2. Mark the four holes through the back of the box with a pencil.

  3. Drill. Into wood siding or a stud the screws go straight in; into brick or block, drill for the masonry anchors and tap them home.

  4. Drive the top two screws, hang the box, check the level again, then drive the bottom two.

  5. Lock it, pocket one key, and put the other somewhere you will remember.

A post at the curb

  1. Have your buried lines marked before you dig. Every state runs a free locating service.

  2. Mark a spot that puts the mailbox door 6 to 8 inches back from the face of the curb or the edge of the road.

  3. Dig 24 inches down and about 8 inches across. Stand the sleeve in the hole with its top level with the ground.

  4. Check the sleeve with a level on two faces, then pack the hole with concrete or tamped gravel. Give concrete a day.

  5. Slide the post in, choose the height that puts the base of the box 41 to 45 inches above the road surface, and fit the bolt.

  6. Bolt the mailbox to the arm with its door toward the road.

About those numbers. The 41 to 45 inch height and the 6 to 8 inch setback are the usual curbside guideline in the US. Routes differ, so if yours has habits of its own, your mail carrier is the person to ask before the concrete goes in. We do not make installation visits; the fitting sheet in the box covers every step above with drawings.
