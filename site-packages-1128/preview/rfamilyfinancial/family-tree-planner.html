The six rules the planner uses, in words

  1. The pedigree rule. Every person has two parents, so each generation back doubles: 2, 4, 8, 16 and on. Counting the child as one, n generations back comes to 2n + 1 − 1 boxes. This is the standard count behind every pedigree chart.
  2. The numbering rule. The child is number 1. A person’s father is double their number and the mother is double plus one. Genealogists call this standard numbering an Ahnentafel, German for “ancestor table.” It gives every ancestor one number, and no two ever collide.
  3. The family rule. Every couple on the chart is one family, and n generations back contains 2n − 1 couples. Include brothers and sisters and each couple gets one family page listing all their children.
  4. The book rule. A record book has 128 record pages. The planner allows one page for each person, adds one for each couple when brothers and sisters are included, divides by 128 and rounds up.
  5. The chart rule. Up to 31 boxes fit the four-generation chart, 63 the five-generation chart and 127 the six-generation fan. Seven generations back is 255 boxes, which the planner splits into two six-generation fans, one for each parent.
  6. The price rule. Each wall chart is $32 and each record book is $58. The total is the charts plus the books. Shipping is added on the order form, where you can change any quantity.

A short guide

How to plan a family tree, in eight steps

  1. Start with one person. Usually the youngest: the child the chart is for. That person is number 1 and sits at the foot of the chart.
  2. Number upward. Use the numbering rule above. Number 1’s father is 2 and mother is 3; number 2’s parents are 4 and 5; and so on up the page.
  3. Fill in what you know, in pencil. Work one generation at a time. Leave a box empty sooner than guess; an empty box is honest and a wrong one is contagious.
  4. Ask the oldest relatives first. Take the chart with you, and write down who told you what. The record book has a log for exactly this.
  1. Write women under the name they were born with. Married names go on the person’s page, where there is room for all of them.
  2. Write dates long and places small to large. “4 July 1931, Lititz, Lancaster County, Pennsylvania” cannot be misread in a hundred years.
  3. Decide about brothers and sisters now. A chart carries the direct line. Siblings belong on family pages in the record book, one page for each couple.
  4. Then choose the paper. The chart is for the wall. The book is for everything that will not fit in a box an inch wide.

The planner counts; it does not research. We do not trace ancestors, search archives or check anyone’s dates. We make the paper and you supply the family.

In the tube

One chart, rolled around a core so it cannot crease, tied with cotton tape, plus a soft pencil for the first draft. Charts ordered with books travel in the same carton.

Filling it in

Pencil first. When a generation is settled, go over it with an archival pigment pen. A ruler under your writing hand keeps the lines level and the ink off your sleeve.
