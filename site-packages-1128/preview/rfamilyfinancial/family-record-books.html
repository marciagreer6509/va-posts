Book one

Family history record book

A record book is the opposite of a shoebox. Everything the family knows about one person goes on that person’s page, and the page has a number, so a name on one page can point to its parents on another.

Each record page has ruled spaces for a full name and the name the family actually used; born, married and died, each with a date and a place; parents and children, with their page numbers; where they lived and what they did; a space marked for a 3½ × 5 in photograph in corner mounts; and fifteen open lines headed “What we know.” An envelope of forty paper photo corners is tucked inside the back cover.

Why 128 record pages

Arithmetic, not marketing. Counting yourself as one, six generations of direct ancestors come to 127 people. A book of 128 record pages holds every one of them, a page each, with a single page left over for the cousin everyone argues about. How a book is bound here

Six steps, the same for the record book and the baby memory book. None of them is quick, and one of them is the reason the book still opens flat when the ink has gone brown.

  1. Fold. Sheets are folded with a bone folder into sections of sixteen pages, with the grain of the paper running head to tail so the pages turn without fighting you.
  2. Tear. The fore-edge of each section is torn against a steel rule, which leaves a deckle edge. The head is trimmed smooth so dust cannot settle in it.
  3. Sew. The sections are sewn through the fold with waxed linen thread, each one linked to the last by a kettle stitch at head and tail. This is the step glued books skip.
  4. Line. The spine takes a strip of cotton mull and a paper lining, set with an acid-free adhesive that stays flexible.
  5. Case. Binder’s board is cut a shade larger than the pages and covered in book cloth. The sewn block is cased in, with the ribbon marker anchored under the spine lining.
  6. Press. The finished book sits between boards under weight overnight. It leaves the press flat, square and slightly smug.

A sewn binding cannot take extra pages later. If a family outgrows one record book, a second volume in the same cloth sits beside it.

Thread, not glue. It is the slow step, and the one that lets a book lie open on the table.

Book two

Baby memory book

Eighty pages for the first five years, with prompts short enough to answer one-handed. It opens on the page most baby books call a birth record. Ours is headed Original Delivery Day.

The Original Delivery Day page

This is the page for the day the baby arrived: the date and the hour, weight and length, who was in the room, what the weather was doing and the first thing anyone said out loud. We call it the Original Delivery Day because every later delivery to your door will be smaller and a good deal less interesting. The page asks only for “the day you arrived,” so it suits a child who came home by any road.

How to write in a book you cannot erase

  • Pencil first. A soft pencil and a light hand. Ink it when two relatives agree.
  • Then pigment ink. Archival pigment pens and fountain-pen inks sold as permanent stay dark. Ordinary ballpoint can fade and print itself onto the facing page.
  • Mount, do not paste. Photo corners hold a print with no adhesive touching it, and the print can come out again when someone finally identifies the man on the left.
  • Write dates long. “4 July 1931,” never “7/4/31.” The short form can be read two ways, and both grandchildren will be sure.
  • Name your sources. The back pages carry a “who told me” log. A fact with a name beside it can be checked; a fact without one becomes a legend.
  • Keep it where people live. A shelf in a lived-in room is kinder to paper than an attic or a basement.
