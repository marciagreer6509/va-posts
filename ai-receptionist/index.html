AI Receptionist for Bookings — US Traction

Appointments in general, with a focus on doctors · research + Google Keyword Planner, 21 Nov 2026 · nothing built

Verdict: the demand is real, the money is pouring in, and it's crowded. Medical AI receptionists already have a $1.2 B startup, a16z-backed rivals and Zocdoc/Phreesia selling their own. The opening for a newcomer is small independent practices (dental, chiro, PT, med-spa, derm, vet): enterprise tools skip them because they're too small, and $29 generic bots don't connect to their scheduling software or sign a HIPAA BAA.

0. Google Keyword Planner — how big is the search demand? (pulled 21 Nov 2026)

Yes, this is a real market that people pay for, and demand jumped this summer. In the US, "ai receptionist" grew from about 5,400 searches a month (Nov 2025–Jun 2026) to 450,000 in Aug and 49,500 in Oct. "ai voice agent(s)" went from about 1,600 to 201,000 and then 14,800. Advertisers bid $14–$67 per click at the top of the page, which only makes sense because these clicks turn into paying customers.

What the numbers say
  • Buyers search the generic phrase, not by niche. "ai receptionist" draws 49k a month, while "…for doctors" gets only 10–210 searches. Doctors searching for this are few, but each click is the most expensive (up to $151). So: generic product, niche landing pages.
  • Builders are a second market. Retell, Vapi and Bland get about 45k searches a month combined. These are agencies building receptionists for clients, so a white-label/reseller plan could sell to them too.
  • Outside the US, demand is ~2% of US demand (UK/CA/AU ~1k each for "ai receptionist"). Start in the US. Other countries are the later stage, as you said.
  • Can a NEW product sell right now? Yes. In 2026 alone ElevenLabs ($29/mo), Pie, Newo and Phonely all launched or raised money and are winning customers in this already-old niche. Competitive pricing works if we stay above the cost floor: voice platforms charge roughly $0.07–0.15 per minute (list prices, to confirm before we set ours).

2. Why practices buy (the pain)

  • Small practices miss up to 30% of calls during office hours (ACAAI), or about 1 in 4 (Solutionreach). Most of those patients don't call back.
  • Calls can't be fully staffed: one front-desk person can take about 4–6 calls an hour while also handling the waiting room.
  • After-hours calls are 14–40% of call volume (estimates vary). One dataset shows after-hours AI calls turn into bookings 23.6% of the time vs 18.6% in office hours.
  • Market size: $2–4.6 B in 2026, growing 10–28% a year depending on the analyst. Definitions differ, so treat this only as a direction.

⚠ Most missed-call and market numbers come from vendors or research resellers. They point the same way but aren't independently verified.

4. Where a newcomer can win

Best wedge: one niche × one booking system. Examples: chiropractors on ChiroTouch/Jane, med-spas on Boulevard/Mindbody, dentists on Dentrix/Open Dental, PT clinics on WebPT. Sell "never miss a new-patient call", priced per booked appointment or as a flat $300–$600/mo.
Must-haves to be credible: a HIPAA BAA · books directly into their software (not "we'll text you a message") · after-hours + overflow mode · hands urgent calls to a human · says it's an AI (state disclosure laws are coming in) · a monthly "calls saved → bookings" report.
Avoid: health systems (Assort, Hyro and Zocdoc own them) and generic "AI receptionist for everyone" (ElevenLabs at $29 kills the margin).
Fast, cheap test: build on a voice platform (Vapi / Retell / ElevenLabs agents), launch in one niche + one booking system, and pitch 50 local practices with a free 14-day missed-call audit. Traction = at least 5 paying in 60 days.
