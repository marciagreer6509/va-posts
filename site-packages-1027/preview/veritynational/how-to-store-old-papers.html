Drawer G · the storage guide

Guide · before you begin

How to store old papers at home

A practical guide for one evening's work: sort, take out what harms, wrap, box and label — then find the right place in the house.

No special room is needed. A clean table, a pencil, a soft brush and enough time to read as you go are the whole kit. The point is not perfection; it is getting papers out of the conditions that slowly harm them and into an order a relative could follow.

Guide · sorting

Step one: sort before you store

  1. Wash and dry your hands and clear the table. Cotton gloves are often recommended but they reduce your grip on thin paper; clean, dry hands are usually safer.
  2. Make rough piles by person, household or subject. Resist the urge to sort strictly by date on the first pass — that comes later, inside each pile.
  3. Keep things together that arrived together. A letter and the envelope it came in, or a bundle a grandparent tied up themselves, carry meaning in their grouping. Note it in pencil on the wallet.
  4. Set aside the fragile: anything torn, brittle, mouldy or stuck together. Do not try to flatten or separate these. Wrap each loosely in tissue and box them on their own.
  5. Decide what is not worth keeping, such as duplicate copies or blank stationery, but go slowly — receipts and ordinary letters are often the papers that tell a family's story best.
Guide · clips and bands

Step two: remove clips, pins and rubber bands

Metal fastenings rust in a damp house, and rust from clips leaves orange stains and holes that spread into the paper. Slide paper clips off sideways rather than pulling them up, supporting the sheet with a flat finger. Staples can be eased out from the back with a blunt knife, opening the legs first.

Rubber bands are worse. As they age they go sticky, then hard, and leave a brown, tacky line where they lay. If a band has perished onto the paper, do not scrape it; leave the residue and wrap the item on its own in tissue.

Where pages genuinely belong together, put them in one document wallet instead of fastening them. A folded slip of tissue between groups inside the wallet does the job of a clip with none of the harm.

Guide · light, damp, temperature

Step three: the enemies at home

Light

Daylight and bright bulbs fade ink and photographs and yellow paper. Keep papers in closed boxes, and display copies rather than originals. A framed original on a sunny wall will fade within a few years.

Damp

Mould grows on paper in damp air, and damp makes ink run and pages stick. Avoid cellars, garages, outside walls and the space under a sink. If a room feels damp to you, it is too damp for papers.

Temperature

Heat speeds every chemical change in paper; swings of heat and cold are worse still. Lofts are hot in summer and cold in winter. A cupboard against an inside wall in a lived-in room is usually best.

The steadiest place in most British homes is a wardrobe or a shelf in a bedroom with no outside wall, away from radiators. Keep boxes off the floor by a few centimetres, in case of a leak, and not directly against a wall, so air moves around them.

Make a simple list of what each box holds and keep a copy outside the box — in a notebook, or a photograph of the list on your phone. A box is only useful to the next generation if they can tell what is in it.
