Boxes · the board

Boxes and folders

Lidded boxes, document wallets and tissue: the three layers between a family's papers and the attic, the damp and the light.

A good storage box does three jobs. It keeps light off, it slows the swing of humidity, and it does not add any harm of its own. Most household boxes fail the third test. Brown corrugated card and shoeboxes are made from wood pulp that still contains lignin, and over the years the lignin breaks down into acids that move into whatever lies against it — you can see it as a brown line on the edge of a letter that has sat against a box wall.

Boxes · terms explained

What acid-free and lignin-free mean

Acid-free describes the board when it leaves the mill: it tests at pH 7 or above, so it is neutral or slightly alkaline rather than acidic. On its own that is not the whole story, because board that still carries lignin can become acidic as it ages. That is why we also insist on lignin-free: the lignin has been removed from the pulp, so there is nothing left inside the board to sour later.

Our boxes and wallets are also buffered. During manufacture a small reserve of calcium carbonate is added to the board. It sits there doing nothing until acids arrive — from the papers themselves, from a newspaper cutting, from polluted air — and then neutralises them. Buffering is the right choice for nearly all paper: letters, certificates, deeds, ration books, programmes, school reports and the like.

A few materials prefer a neutral, unbuffered surround: colour photographs, blueprints, and some early photographic prints. The simplest way to handle them is to sleeve them in polyester first and then box the sleeves; the polyester is inert and keeps the print away from the board. The sleeves page explains this in more detail.

Sizes, lids and what fits where

Both boxes are two-piece: a base and a separate lid with a full drop, so the lid comes most of the way down the sides. That gives a double wall all round and a dust seal at the top edge. Boxes · inside the box

Wallets, labelling and stacking

A box full of loose sheets is still a muddle. Document wallets bring order inside it: one wallet per person, per house, per decade or per subject, whatever makes sense to the family. Each is a single folded sheet of acid-free card with a tuck flap, so nothing slides out when the box is tipped.

Label in soft pencil, never in ballpoint or felt pen, which can bleed through damp and fade unevenly. Write on the wallet, not on the papers. On the box, a pencilled label on the end panel lets you read the contents when boxes are stacked on a shelf.

Stack boxes no more than four high, the heaviest at the bottom, and keep them flat rather than on end so the papers rest on their faces instead of their edges.

Boxes · tissue

Interleaving with tissue

Tissue goes between items that should not touch: a newspaper cutting and the letter it was sent with, a folded certificate and its neighbour, a pressed flower and anything at all. Newsprint in particular is acidic and will brown the paper beside it, so a sheet of buffered tissue either side costs pennies and spares the letter a permanent mark.

Our sheets are 500 × 750 mm, large enough to cut down for most jobs. Cut with scissors rather than tearing, so no loose fibres catch on fragile edges. Tissue is also useful for padding the empty corners of a part-filled box so the papers do not slide when it is moved.
