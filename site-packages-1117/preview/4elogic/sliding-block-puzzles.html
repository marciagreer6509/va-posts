Sliding blocks and puzzle boxes

Puzzles counted in moves

A sliding-block tray and a secret-opening box look nothing alike, but both are solved the same way: a sequence of moves, in the right order, with no shortcuts.

The sliding-block tray

Our tray holds ten rectangular blocks in a closed frame with one exit slot. The large square block starts at the top; the aim is to slide it down and out through the slot. You may only slide blocks into empty space, never lift or turn them, so every move opens one gap and closes another.

It looks simple and is not. The blocks jam each other in ways that are hard to see from the starting position, and the shortest solution is long enough that most people need several attempts before the route becomes clear. We grade it Steady to Stubborn: the first half yields to patience, the second half to planning.

Each block is cut from hardwood and waxed on every face, and the tray floor is waxed too, so blocks slide with a fingertip rather than catching on the grain. The sealed card lists the solution as a numbered move sequence with small diagrams every few steps.

Secret-opening puzzle boxes

A puzzle box looks like a plain lidded box until you try to open it. The lid will not lift because something inside is holding it, and the way in is a chain of hidden actions: a side panel that slides a few millimetres, a pin that drops when the box is tilted, a drawer that is free only after the panel has moved. The number of moves is the number of separate actions needed before the box opens.

The three-move box is the gentle introduction. Each action is a real discovery, but the chain is short enough to solve in an evening, and once open it makes a handsome small box for keeping things in. We grade it Warm-up.

The nine-move box is a different animal. Actions must happen in a strict order, some undo earlier ones, and one step only works with the box held a particular way up. It is graded Fiendish, and the sealed card inside the outer sleeve is the only one we recommend keeping somewhere you cannot reach easily.

Counting moves

In a tray, one move is one block slid any distance in one direction. In a box, one move is one hidden action. Both sealed cards count the same way, so the number on the card matches the number in the name.

Nothing is forced

No step in our boxes needs strength, a tool or a magnet. If something resists, the order is wrong. That rule protects the joinery and keeps the puzzle fair.

Which to start with

New to move puzzles: the three-move box or the tray. Bought the twelve-piece burr and still hungry: the nine-move box. Ages 8 and up for all three.
