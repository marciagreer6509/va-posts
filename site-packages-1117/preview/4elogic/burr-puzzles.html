Burr puzzles

Six sticks, one knot, and a key piece that slides last

A burr is a set of notched square sticks that lock into a symmetrical knot. Ours are cut from beech and walnut and arrive assembled, so the first job is taking one apart.

How a burr interlocks

Every burr is built around the same trick. Each stick has notches cut into it, and the notches of one stick hug the solid parts of the sticks that cross it. Three pairs of sticks run along the three directions of a cube, so every stick is trapped by others. The whole knot only holds because one piece, the key piece, has no notch where the others need it and can therefore slide out first. Pull the key and the rest come apart in an order; put them back and the key goes in last.

That last move is the satisfying one. The knot is loose and wobbly with five sticks in place; then the key piece pushes through the gap that was left for it and the whole thing becomes a rigid star. If you are taking it apart for the first time, look for the stick that moves a little more freely than the rest. That is usually where it starts.

Piece counts and what they change

The six-piece burr is the classic form: six sticks, three directions, one key. Different notch patterns give different puzzles, and the beech version we cut has a single internal void, so it comes apart in a short sequence but reassembles only if you remember which stick faces which way. Most solvers take it apart in minutes and put it back after a proper think.

The twelve-piece burr doubles the sticks, laying a second shell of six over the first. The notches have to agree in two layers at once, so pieces must often move partway, let another past, then move again. It is a much longer assembly, and the grade reflects that.

A see-through study burr shows what happens inside: the notches meet in the middle, and the empty space is exactly where the key piece slides.

Pale and near-black timber side by side: the same contrast that makes walnut joints easy to read against beech.

Woods and grades

Beech for the six, walnut for the twelve

Beech

Pale, close-grained and very stable once seasoned. It holds a crisp internal corner, which keeps the six-piece joints snug without forcing, and the light colour makes each notch easy to read while you work.

Walnut

Darker, with a soft sheen after waxing. On the twelve-piece burr the colour helps: you can see each stick against its neighbours, which matters when two layers of notches have to line up at the same moment.

Grades used here

The six-piece sits at Warm-up for taking apart and Steady for putting back. The twelve-piece is graded Stubborn: expect several sessions, and expect to move sticks halfway rather than all at once.

How we cut them

Square stock is planed, left to rest so any movement in the wood happens before cutting, then cut to length against a stop. The notches are made on jigs set for each design, one stick at a time, and each finished stick is test-fitted against the others before the set is waxed. A set that binds is reworked or set aside; a set that rattles goes in the offcut bin.

Every burr is shipped assembled, wrapped, with its solution card sealed in an envelope. The card draws the disassembly as numbered diagrams and the assembly as the same steps in reverse.

Choosing between them

New to burrs? Start with the six-piece beech: it teaches how the key piece works and rewards a careful look at the notches. Already own a few? The twelve-piece walnut is the one to keep on the desk for a week. Both are for ages 8 and up, and both sit happily on a shelf as solid wooden objects once solved.
