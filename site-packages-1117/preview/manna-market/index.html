What we stock

Five tool lines, nothing else

No flour, no starter, no bread. Just the gear that sits between your mixing bowl and your oven.

Round and oval bannetons

Coiled rattan cane that holds the shape of a high-hydration dough through a long, cold proof. Each basket comes with a fitted linen liner, and we list it by the dough weight it suits, not just its diameter.

Flax linen couches

Heavy, unbleached flax that you pleat between baguettes so they rise up rather than out. A light rub of rice flour into the weave and the dough lets go cleanly when you roll it onto the peel.

Bread lames with spare blades

A slim wooden handle that grips a double-edged blade, so you can score at a shallow angle and coax a proper ear. Five blades ride along in the box, because a dull edge drags rather than cuts.

Bench and bowl scrapers

A rigid steel bench blade for dividing and pre-shaping, and a flexible bowl scraper that follows the curve of a mixing bowl. Sold as a pair because every bake needs both.

Linen bread bags

A drawstring flax bag that lets the crust breathe for the first day or two. It shakes out, washes cool and dries on the line, ready for the next loaf.

How we work

A small shop that weighs everything

Manna Market is a small online shop in Bendigo selling the tools a home sourdough baker reaches for once the starter is bubbling and the first few loaves have spread flat across the tray. We keep the range narrow on purpose. Five tool lines, a starter kit and a price list short enough to read in one go.

Every listing tells you the size, what it is made of and the loaf it suits. We write bannetons up by dough weight because that is how bakers actually plan a bake: you know your recipe makes about 900 g of dough, so you need a basket that holds 900 g without the dough cresting the rim. Couches are listed by how many baguettes they will pleat, bags by the size of loaf they close around.

An order runs simply. You fill in the order form with the tools and quantities you want and where they should go. We count the stock on the shelf, then write back with a tallied-up figure and a card link to pay through. No money moves until you click it. Once paid, the parcel is packed in Bendigo, usually within two working days, with a printed care note for each tool in the box, and you get the tracking number by e-mail.

What we value is the boring stuff done properly: cane that is smooth inside so it does not snag a proving dough, linen with a tight enough weave that rice flour sits on it rather than through it, blades that arrive sharp. What we turn down: flour, starter and bread, since we are not a bakery; baking classes; custom engraving on handles or baskets; and post to addresses outside Australia.

Questions at the bench

Things bakers ask us

Go by the weight of the dough, not the flour. The 22 cm round suits a dough of roughly 500 to 750 g, which is the usual one-loaf bake from about 400 to 500 g of flour. The 28 cm oval takes 750 g up to about 1 kg and gives a longer batard that slices evenly for sandwiches. If the dough sits well below the rim at the start of the proof, the basket is about right; if it crowns over the top, go up a size.

Not always. Bare cane, dusted with rice flour, prints the classic spiral rings onto the crust. The liner is kinder to very wet doughs and to a new basket that has not built up its seasoning of flour yet, and it is easier to keep clean. Both our bannetons ship with a linen liner, so you can try it both ways and keep whichever your dough likes.

Mostly you do not wash it. Tap the basket out, brush the flour from between the coils with a stiff dry brush and let it air dry fully, ideally in sun, before it goes back in the cupboard. If dough has stuck, let it dry hard and brush it off. Liners go through a cool wash and dry flat. A damp basket kept closed up is what turns musty, so dryness is the whole trick.
