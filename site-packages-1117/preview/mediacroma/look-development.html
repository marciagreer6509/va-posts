Reference frames

Start from reference frames

A look is a decision about light, and the fastest way to share one is with pictures. We ask for a handful of reference frames: stills from films, photographs, paintings you have photographed in a gallery, a frame from your own tests. Five to fifteen is a good number; more than that and the references begin to argue with each other.

For each frame, a line on what you are drawn to. "The green in the shadows." "How the skin is warm but the room is cold." "Black that is not quite black." Those sentences matter more than the frames themselves, because the same still can be loved for opposite reasons.

We read the references on the scopes as well as by eye. A frame that feels cold may have perfectly neutral highlights and only blue-shifted shadows; a frame that feels dense may simply be lower in midtone saturation. Measuring them turns a mood board into a set of decisions we can reproduce.

Test LUTs

From references to three test LUTs

  • Camera tests from your cinematographerA short test on the actual camera, lens and log setting you will shoot with: a face, a grey card, a colour chart if you have one, and the kind of light the film lives in. Ten minutes of footage is plenty.
  • Three looks, built on that footageNot three variations of one idea, but three distinct directions drawn from your references, each built over a technically correct transform from the camera's log to Rec.709.
  • A review callWe walk through the three looks on a shared stream, on the same frames, and talk about what each does to skin, to darkness and to the colours of your locations.
  • Test LUTs exportedEach look is exported as a LUT in the common cube format, sized for monitors and recorders on set, with a short note on the exposure each one expects.

A test LUT lets the cinematographer, the director and the production designer see the intended look on the monitor while they shoot. It changes decisions on the day: how hard to light a wall, whether a costume reads, how far to let a window blow out.

What a test LUT does not do is replace the grade. It is a single, fixed transform applied to every shot the same way; it cannot balance a cloudy take against a sunny one or pull a face out of a dark corner. When the edit is locked, the look you chose becomes the starting point of the grade, rebuilt as adjustable nodes rather than a baked-in file, and refined shot by shot.

We always ask the camera team to record clean log with the LUT applied to the monitor output only. A look burnt into the camera file cannot be undone in the suite.

When look development is worth it

When a film's look depends on choices made on the day: practical light colour, wardrobe and set colour, how dark a scene can be shot. When several departments need to agree on a picture before money is spent on it. When a director and cinematographer are working together for the first time and want to see whether they mean the same thing by "warm".

When it is not

When the film is already shot. Then the look is developed inside the grade, at no extra charge, in the first pass. We will tell you so rather than sell you a separate step.
