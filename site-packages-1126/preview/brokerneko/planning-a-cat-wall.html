The workshop's rule of thumb

The spacing rule, in words

This is the rule the planner follows. It is the workshop's own rule of thumb from building and living with these walls, not a standard of any kind, and you should adjust it for the cat you know.

  1. Rises of about 30 to 40 cm. The planner takes the height of the top piece and divides it into the fewest equal rises it can without any rise being taller than 40 cm. The first rise is from the floor. On most walls that lands every step between 30 and 40 cm; a short climb to a low sill can come out a little under.
  2. Steps no wider than a comfortable jump. Each shelf is offset sideways from the one below by 50 cm, centre to centre. With 40 cm shelves that leaves a 10 cm gap between edges, so the next step is always a short hop up and across, never a leap.
  3. Sitting room at the top. On the "up and across" and "loop" routes the highest pieces sit 45 cm under the ceiling, enough for a cat to sit upright on the lookout.
  4. A margin at the sides. Nothing is placed within 15 cm of either end of the wall, which keeps shelves clear of corners and door frames.
  5. Bridges join level pieces. A 90 cm bridge always runs between two shelves at one height, with a 10 cm gap at each end. That is why a bridge needs at least 220 cm of wall, and why the planner leaves it out on narrower walls and tells you so.
  6. The window perch replaces the last step. On the window route the perch platform is set at sill height at the right-hand end of the wall and the shelves climb up to it.

When a staircase reaches the margin it turns round and zigzags back, so a narrow wall gets a ladder-like climb and a wide wall gets a long diagonal.

Choosing a route

Three routes and when each one suits

Up and across

The classic. A run of step shelves climbs to just under the ceiling, a bridge carries on along the top and a last shelf makes the lookout. Choose it for a living-room wall where you want the route to be seen. It uses the most shelves of the three.

To the window

The modest one. One or two shelves and a window perch, all below sill height. Choose it for a bedroom, a rental where you want as few holes as possible, or as a first buy to see whether the idea takes.

Loop

The busy one. Two staircases joined by a bridge at the top, so there is a way up and a different way down. Choose it for two cats, since nobody gets cornered at a dead end, or for one cat who likes a circuit.

Adding a post

Tick the scratching-post box and the planner stands one beside the first shelf. It gives the route a rough-textured start and gives claws something to do that is not your sofa.

Before the drill comes out

Lining a route up on studs

The planner draws on a blank wall. Yours has timber studs behind the plasterboard, and screws belong in those. Here is how to get from the drawing to pencil marks.

  1. Find the studs first. Run a stud finder along the wall, or knock and listen for the dull spots, and mark each stud lightly in pencil at about chest height. They are commonly 45 or 60 cm apart.
  2. Shift the whole route, not one shelf. Hold the drawing against your marks. Slide the entire layout left or right until as many brackets as possible fall on studs. The planner's 15 cm side margin gives you room to move.
  3. Nudge the stragglers. A shelf whose brackets still miss can move up to 10 cm sideways; the gap to its neighbour grows or shrinks a little and the hop stays short.
  4. Set heights from the list. The drawing labels each level in centimetres from the floor. Mark the first, hang that shelf, and measure each following rise from the shelf below.
  5. Brick or block walls skip the stud hunt: drill, plug and screw wherever the drawing says.

We do not give weight figures for walls we have not seen. If you are unsure what is behind your plasterboard, have someone who knows the building look before you drill, and if you rent, get your landlord's written permission.
