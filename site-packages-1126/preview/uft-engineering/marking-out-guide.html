How to mark out a part.

A plain method for getting lines and hole centres onto metal with a surface plate, a square, a scriber and two punches. It is the guide we print and put in every parcel. Further down is the tap drill calculator, for the moment after the punch and before the drill.

You will need

  • Engineer's square to suit the part

  • Parallels and a clamp for awkward shapes

The method

Eight steps from bare metal to a punched centre

The example is a flat steel bracket with two tapped holes, but the order of work is the same for a cylinder cover or a locomotive frame. The whole idea is to choose two datum edges and take every dimension from them.

  1. Make two datum edgesFile or machine one long edge straight, then one short edge square to it. Check with the square held to the light. Mark both edges with a felt pen so you never measure from the wrong side.
  2. Clean and blueDegrease the face and brush on a thin coat of layout blue. Thin is the word: a thick coat flakes and takes the line with it. Give it a minute to dry.
  3. Stand the work on the plateWipe the plate, then set the part on its long datum edge. If it will not stand, clamp it to something square, such as an angle plate or a parallel on edge.
  4. Scribe the heightsSet a height gauge or scribing block to each dimension measured up from the datum edge and draw it across the face. No height gauge yet? Rest a steel rule upright against the square, set the scriber to it on a parallel, and slide.
  5. Turn the work and scribe the other wayStand the part on its short datum edge and scribe the dimensions measured from that edge. Each hole centre is now a crossing of two lines.
  6. Check before you punchMeasure the crossings against the drawing with a rule. A scribed line can be blued over and done again. A punch mark cannot.
  7. Dot punch, then centre punchLean the dot punch over, drag its point along one line until it clicks into the other, stand it upright and tap. Look at the dot through a glass. If it is off, lean the punch and tap it across. Then open it with the centre punch.
  8. Box the holeFor an important hole, scribe a circle of the finished size round the centre with dividers and dot it at four points. As the drill starts, you can see whether it is running true inside the circle and draw it over while there is still time.

Three habits worth having

Scribe each line once, with the point trailing. Keep the plate free of filings; one chip under the work tilts every line. And write the datum edges on the drawing, so that next week you measure from the same two faces.

Tap drill calculator

Pick a metric coarse thread from M2 to M12, or type your own diameter and pitch. The answer updates as you type.

The rule, in words. For a metric thread the tapping drill is the major diameter minus the pitch. That is the standard workshop rule and it leaves roughly three-quarters of a full thread, which is strong and taps without a fight.

Nearest drill. Drills are commonly sold in 0.1 mm steps, so the answer is rounded to the nearest tenth. An exact half goes up to the larger drill: slightly less thread, a much happier tap.

Thread percentage. By the usual formula: (major diameter − drill) ÷ (1.299 × pitch).

Rule in use: drill = major diameter − pitch.
Pick a thread and both boxes fill themselves. Type in either box to try a fine pitch or an odd size; a comma or a full stop both work as the decimal mark.

In tough materials, or with a small tap you would rather not break, going up one more tenth is common practice and costs little strength. In soft aluminium or brass the listed size is fine. Always start the tap square to the face: stand the 50 mm square on the work beside the tap and sight it from two directions.
