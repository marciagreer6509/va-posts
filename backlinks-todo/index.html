That is the thinking behind an oral spray. VL Plus is a food supplement that goes straight into the mouth from a small bottle, with no tablet to swallow. The 30 ml bottle has a blackberry flavour and is sweetened with steviol glycosides, so sugar is not on the ingredient list. One bottle is meant to last 30 days, which means the bottle itself works as a calendar: when it is empty, a month has gone by.

It also helps to know what is actually inside. A daily dose of VL Plus contains vitamin C, zinc, six B vitamins and biotin, together with three amino compounds, namely L-citrulline, L-glutamine and L-carnitine. Reading a supplement label: what the vitamin names really mean

Supplement labels can look like a chemistry exam. Words such as pyridoxine, methylcobalamin or gluconate appear in small print, and it is easy to wonder whether they are good news or just jargon. Once you know the pattern, however, most of them turn out to be familiar vitamins wearing their formal names.

Take the ingredient list of VL Plus Spray as a worked example for readers in Ireland. Vitamin C is listed as L-ascorbic acid and sodium ascorbate, two forms of the same vitamin. Zinc is added as zinc gluconate. Vitamin B6 appears as pyridoxine HCl, and vitamin B12 as methylcobalamin. Niacin, pantothenic acid, riboflavin and thiamine are simply the names of vitamins B3, B5, B2 and B1, and biotin is biotin.

Order matters as well. Ingredients are listed from the largest share to the smallest, which is why water and the sweetener, steviol glycosides, come first in a liquid spray, and why the small amounts of B12 and biotin come last. Further down you will see natural blackberry flavour, potassium sorbate and a thickener called xanthan gum, all of which have an everyday job: taste, keeping the liquid stable and giving it body.

The table of nutrients per daily dose adds a second layer. The B vitamins and biotin reach the full nutrient reference value, zinc covers a large share of it and vitamin C about half. L-citrulline, L-glutamine and L-carnitine have no reference value, so they carry a dash instead of a percentage. Four authorised health claims apply to the vitamins and zinc, covering tiredness and fatigue, energy-yielding metabolism, the immune system and hair.

A tidy label does not make a supplement suitable for everyone. Check with a doctor or pharmacist if you are pregnant, breastfeeding or taking medication, and remember that it is no stand-in for a varied diet.
